The forward deployed engineer, or FDE, is a software engineer who works inside a customer's organization to turn complex technology into production systems. The role borrows from product engineering, solutions architecture, and consulting, but one person owns the whole path from discovery to adoption in one account. Palantir pioneered the model. In [AI](/ai), that embedded pattern has become one of the most hired roles in the industry, and I think it's a good signal of where the hard part of AI actually sits.

## Why the role matters now

Demos now outrun production. Models look capable in slides, while customers still need integration, governance, and someone who understands how the business runs. Deploying an agent is often harder than shipping conventional software, because you're changing how people do their work.

That gap shows up in hiring. AI startups are embedding engineers with accounts to wire integrations and make models work where the defaults fail, and FDE postings have grown sharply as vendors treat adoption as the constraint. OpenAI, Google Cloud, Anthropic, ServiceNow, and Accenture have expanded similar programs. The companies selling AI are adding engineers to staff the last mile. If you want the market view, a16z's [piece on the Palantirization of everything](https://a16z.com/the-palantirization-of-everything/), [PostHog's look at FDE hiring](https://posthog.com/blog/forward-deployed-engineer), [IBM's conversation](https://www.ibm.com/think/insights/conversation-forward-deployed-engineers-incomplete) on AI impact stuck in pilots, and [Aaron Levie on X](https://x.com/levie/status/2054398342852194386) are all worth reading.

## What an FDE does

An FDE owns technical success where the software meets the organization, from discovery through adoption inside one customer's environment.

The work starts with conversations. FDEs talk to users, operators, executives, and internal engineering teams until the real workflow, constraints, and outcomes are clear. That takes iteration, because people often don't know what they want until they see something they don't want, as NetBox Labs co-founder Mark Coleman put it.

Then they build. They write software, wire integrations, configure platforms, and adapt the vendor product to the customer's databases, APIs, policies, and legacy tools. That's normal [fullstack](/fullstack) work on a customer-specific timeline, often measured in weeks on site rather than quarters on a roadmap.

The strong ones avoid endless one-off customization. They spot patterns that should become platform features or better defaults, help teams use what shipped, validate impact, and feed what broke in the field back to the product team.

## What it takes

The bar is production AI systems, not notebook demos: RAG pipelines, evaluation, permissions, observability, and integration into existing stacks. Underneath that sit CS foundations, systems thinking, business context, and fluency with coding agents, MCP, and the tooling around them. Aaron Levie's [skill checklist](https://x.com/levie/status/205472996663044100) is a good one.

The role also rewards tolerance for ambiguity, clear writing, and caring whether the system is used next month, not only whether it compiled today. Much of that overlaps with what I wrote in [Engineering in the AI Era](/blog/2026-02-engineering-ai-era): less syntax, more judgment and review. FDE work adds accountability for adoption in someone else's environment, including [institutional](/institutions) constraints that never appear in a sandbox.

## Where the role goes next

As teams become AI-fluent, I expect many organizations to bring embedded deployment in-house, and vendor-led forward deployed programs may shrink. The deployment work moves inside the customer and stays, because someone still has to take the system from the demo to the team that runs it on Monday morning.
